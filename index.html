eLife · 2025 · Research article

A consequence
for the genome.

Question. How does nuclear-pore remodeling contribute to chromosome loss in aging yeast?

Result. Our 2025 study linked nuclear-basket displacement to pre-mRNA leakage and chromosome loss. Removing introns from three chromosome-segregation genes suppressed the chromosome-loss phenotype.

Why it matters. A structural change at the nuclear pore is connected to an intron-dependent failure of cell function.

02 The questions

A cell is more than its genes.
It matters how it handles their messages.

My current PhD research asks how nuclear RNA quality control changes during aging and stress. A mother yeast cell produces successive daughters; the number produced over its lifetime is its replicative lifespan.

New to RNA biology? Start here.

RNA carries genetic instructions from DNA toward the machinery that makes proteins. Many newly made RNA messages contain segments called introns, which are normally removed by a process called splicing. Cells also control which messages can leave the nucleus.

I study how this processing and quality control change with age. The immediate aim is to understand mechanisms of aging in budding yeast. Whether a finding applies to human aging is a separate experimental question.

Explore my current research questions
I.

Which RNAs are allowed to leave?

How are incompletely processed transcripts retained in the nucleus, and what allows them to escape? The distinction between processing an RNA and controlling its export is central to the question.

RNA surveillance / Nuclear retention
II.

What changes at the nuclear pore?

The nuclear pore connects the nucleus with the rest of the cell. I study how changes in this architecture relate to RNA quality control as a cell grows older.

Nuclear architecture / Aging
III.

When does a molecular change affect the cell?

A molecular change is not yet an explanation. The aim is to distinguish correlation from cause, and connect altered RNA handling to a measurable consequence for the cell.

Mechanism / Single-cell biology

03 How I do the work

Two views of the same problem.
A cell’s life. Its molecular state.

One approach follows the life of an individual cell. The other collects aging cells in sufficient numbers to measure their molecules. Together, they let us study aging at two different scales.

The life of one cell

Watch a cell grow old.

Question
When does a cell’s behavior change during its replicative life?
Approach
Microfluidic traps and repeated imaging follow individual yeast mothers as they produce daughters.
Readout
Division histories and replicative lifespan; fluorescence trajectories where a suitable reporter is used.

The molecules of a population

Collect aging cells.
Measure what changes.

Question
Which molecular features differ between younger and age-enriched yeast populations?
Approach
The Miniature-chemostat Aging Device (MAD) retains magnetically labelled mothers while daughters wash out.
Readout
Age-enriched populations for RNA and other molecular measurements, rather than perfectly age-pure samples.

These approaches address complementary scales: the bulk samples are not the same cells followed in the movies.

From movies to reviewed cell histories.

I develop image-analysis workflows to organize trap-level images, support annotation, and extract division and lifespan summaries. Automated calls remain subject to human review; the workflow is still being developed and validated.

I learned the MAD approach with the Caudron laboratory in Montpellier and worked with the ETH workshop on our laboratory implementation. With thanks to Remo Zangger and Daniel Smith for the setup, and to Sung Sik Lee for the aging-chip platform. MAD was introduced by Hendrickson and colleagues, eLife (2018).
